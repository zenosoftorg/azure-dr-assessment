# Azure SQL Database

## Mechanisms

### Failover groups
- Replicates: data (all or a chosen subset of the user databases of one logical server) asynchronously to a logical server in another region, and gives the group a read-write and a read-only listener endpoint; failover groups build on top of active geo-replication
- Paired: yes
- Nonpaired: yes
- Failover: customer-managed
- Estimated RPO: unknown (Learn: replication is asynchronous, so "it's possible to have data loss when a failover occurs", and "You can estimate the amount of data loss by monitoring the replication lag"; this page publishes no fixed figure, and the business continuity table states an RPO of "Equal to or greater than 0" that depends on the data not yet replicated, so the option sets a replication-lag bound that TC-2 proves)
- Estimated RTO: 60s (Learn: "typically up to 60 seconds of downtime during a failover" for a customer-initiated failover, and "Typically less than 60 seconds" in the business continuity table https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo; this is the downtime of the database failover only, and the time to detect the outage, to decide and to bring dependent application components up in the DR region is not in it, so TC-3 measures the whole switch-over)
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#failover-groups

### Active geo-replication
- Replicates: data (one database at a time, as a continuously synchronized readable secondary database, also called a geo-secondary or geo-replica, on any logical server other than the primary's), asynchronously
- Paired: yes
- Nonpaired: yes
- Failover: customer-managed
- Estimated RPO: unknown (Learn: replication is asynchronous, so "data loss is possible when a failover occurs"; this page publishes no fixed figure, and the business continuity table states an RPO of "Equal to or greater than 0" that depends on the data not yet replicated, so the option sets a replication-lag bound that TC-2 proves)
- Estimated RTO: 60s (Learn: "typically up to 60 seconds of downtime during a failover", and "Typically less than 60 seconds" in the business continuity table https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo; Learn also says that how quickly you reconfigure your application to connect to the new primary database affects the downtime, because active geo-replication has no listener endpoint that follows the primary, so TC-3 measures the whole switch-over)
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#active-geo-replication

### Geo-restore
- Replicates: nothing continuously; it creates a new database on any logical server in any Azure region from the most recent geo-replicated backups of the source database
- Paired: yes
- Nonpaired: yes
- Failover: manual
- Estimated RPO: unknown (Learn: geo-restore recovers "to the point of the most recent replicated backups"; Geo-Redundant Storage replicates asynchronously, and "Typically, the replication latency for GRS is in the range of minutes, but it is not guaranteed to be within a specific time frame")
- Estimated RTO: unknown (Learn: "For a large or very active database in the service tiers other than Hyperscale, the restore might take several hours", and the recovery time of individual databases can increase when a prolonged outage in a region causes many geo-restore requests; no fixed figure)
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore

### Geo-redundant backup storage
- Replicates: the automated backups (point-in-time restore and long-term retention) of a database, asynchronously, to a single physical location in the paired secondary region; geo-zone-redundant storage (GZRS) does the same with zone redundancy in the primary region
- Paired: yes
- Nonpaired: no
- Failover: not applicable
- Estimated RPO: unknown (Learn describes an asynchronous copy of the backups to the paired region and gives no figure on this page)
- Estimated RTO: unknown (the storage has no failover of its own: the backups are used by geo-restore, whose recovery time is its own estimate)
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/automated-backups-overview?view=azuresql

## Constraints

### Failover groups and active geo-replication reach any region
Learn says that active geo-replication "can be enabled in all Azure regions and doesn't require you to use Azure region pairs", and that failover groups "can be created across all Azure regions and don't require you to use Azure region pairs". Active geo-replication can create secondary databases in the same region, but that configuration "doesn't provide protection against a region outage", so the geo-secondary is placed in a different region from the primary.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#resilience-to-region-wide-failures

### Paired regions are recommended for performance and are not required
For failover groups Learn says to use paired regions "as failover groups in paired regions have better performance compared to unpaired regions". Azure SQL Database generally does not update paired regions at the same time, and for geo-replication or failover groups that do not align with the Azure region pairing Learn says to use different maintenance window schedules for the primary and secondary databases, for example a Weekday window for the secondary and a Weekend window for the primary. For a DR region that is not the pair, this means the DR server gets a maintenance window that differs from the primary's.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#use-paired-regions

### Private Link for a failover group is documented for paired regions
Learn's procedure to use a private link with a failover group starts with "Ensure your primary and secondary servers are in a paired region", then asks for a virtual network and subnet in each region with nonoverlapping IP address spaces, a private endpoint and a private DNS zone for the primary server, and a private endpoint for the secondary server that reuses the same private DNS zone. Learn gives no procedure for a nonpaired secondary region, so the private link path to a DR region that is not the pair is built from these steps and proved in a drill (TC-6), not taken as documented.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-configure-sql-db?view=azuresql#use-private-link

### Failover group limits
Failover groups can only be created between logical servers in the same Microsoft Entra tenant, and not between two servers in the same Azure region. They support geo-replication of all databases of the group to only one secondary logical server in a different region, cannot be renamed (delete the group and create it again with another name), and removing a failover group does not stop replication and does not delete the replicated database. The Azure portal does not support creating failover groups across different subscriptions, so PowerShell cmdlets are used for that.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-configure-sql-db?view=azuresql#limitations

### Multiple secondaries for failover groups are a preview
Learn describes up to four secondary servers for a failover group, in the same or in different regions, as a preview feature "that is not recommended for production workloads". Without it, a failover group has one secondary server, so a second DR region is built with active geo-replication on top of the group, not with a second group target.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#multiple-secondaries

### Failover group names are global and listener records are deleted with the group
The name of a failover group must be globally unique within the .database.windows.net domain. When a failover group is deleted, its listener DNS records are deleted too, and there is a non-zero probability that somebody else creates a failover group or a server DNS alias with the same name, so Learn advises against generic failover group names.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-configure-sql-db?view=azuresql#change-the-primary-region

### The failover policy is customer managed or Microsoft managed
Failover groups have two failover policies. The customer-managed policy (recommended; value manual in command line tools and the REST API) lets the customer fail over the group on an outage. With the Microsoft-managed policy (value automatic), Microsoft initiates failover of all affected failover groups that have this policy, and "won't" initiate it for individual failover groups or a subset of them in a region. Learn says to use the customer-managed policy "to test and implement your disaster recovery plan" and not to rely on the Microsoft-managed failover.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#failover-policy

### A Microsoft-managed failover is a forced failover after a grace period of at least one hour
For the Azure SQL service to initiate a forced failover, a region-level outage must affect many databases and the grace period must have expired; "the grace period can't be set below one hour". Learn says the actual time of the forced failover "can vary significantly", that all databases of the group fail over regardless of their zone redundancy or availability, that an unknown amount of data loss is accepted because the synchronization status of the secondaries is ignored, and that the primary and secondary replicas should have the same service tier, compute tier and compute size. A Microsoft-managed failover is an event in the activity log named "Failover Azure SQL failover group" with "Event initiated by" showing a single hyphen.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#microsoft-managed

### Microsoft-initiated failover is likely to come hours after the outage
In the failover groups section, Learn says that "Microsoft triggers failover for all failover groups in the affected region", that Microsoft-managed failovers are forced failovers "which means that some data loss is expected", that the amount of data loss cannot be controlled, and that "the Microsoft response time will likely be several hours at least" even with the minimum grace period of one hour. Microsoft expects to perform this type of failover only in exceptional situations. This means that the plan does not rest on it: Learn's own guidance is to use the customer-managed policy for testing and for the DR plan, and the time of a Microsoft-initiated failover is not one the customer sets (inference from the cited statements; Learn describes no customer drill of it).
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#failover-groups

### Virtual network rules prevent the Microsoft-managed failover policy
Each virtual network service endpoint applies to only one Azure region, so sessions from a client outside the new region can fail after a geo-failover. For that reason "the Microsoft managed failover policy can't be enabled if the participating servers are included in the Virtual Network rules". To support the manual (customer-managed) policy with such rules, Learn's steps are: provision redundant copies of the front-end components in the secondary region, configure the virtual network rules individually for the primary and the secondary server, enable front-end failover with a Traffic Manager configuration, and initiate a manual geo-failover when the outage is detected.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-configure-sql-db?view=azuresql#failover-groups-and-network-security

### The geo-secondary needs the same service tier and should match the primary
"Both the primary and geo-secondary are required to have the same service tier." Learn strongly recommends the same backup storage redundancy, compute tier (provisioned or serverless) and compute size as the primary: a geo-secondary with a lower compute size might not keep up under a heavy write workload, which causes replication lag and might eventually make the geo-secondary unavailable, and active geo-replication throttles the primary's transaction log rate if necessary. After a failover, a smaller new primary can need a scale-up that takes significant time and ends with a high availability failover. When scaling up, scale up the geo-secondary first and then the primary; when scaling down, do the reverse.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#configure-geo-secondary

### Failover group secondaries are created on a server without a same-named database
When a single database is added to a failover group, a secondary database is created with the same edition and compute size on the secondary server, and "the secondary logical server doesn't have a database with the same name" unless it is an existing secondary database. If the primary database is in an elastic pool, the secondary server must hold an elastic pool with the same exact name and enough free capacity. A database with in-memory OLTP objects needs a geo-secondary with a matching service tier, because a lower tier might cause out-of-memory issues and failed failovers.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#terminology-and-capabilities

### Zone redundancy of the secondary is not set by the failover group
For databases other than Hyperscale, secondary databases created through a failover group do not have high availability (zone redundancy) enabled by default, and it is enabled after the group is created. For Hyperscale the secondary inherits the setting of the primary. When the primary has zone redundancy and the secondary region does not support availability zones, adding the database fails with error code 45122, and Learn's workaround is to use active geo-replication, where zone redundancy is enabled or disabled while the secondary is created, and then optionally add the databases to a failover group. This matters for a DR region that is not the pair, because it can differ in zone support.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#enable-high-availability-zone-redundancy

### Initial seeding takes time
When databases are added to a failover group there is an initial seeding phase before replication starts, "the longest and most expensive operation". Under normal circumstances the possible seeding speed is "up to 500 GB an hour for SQL Database", seeding runs for all databases in parallel, and its duration depends on the size of the data, the number of databases, the load on the primary and the speed of the network link between the regions. So the seeding time of a DR region far from the primary has to be observed, not assumed.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#initial-seeding

### The number of databases in a group affects the failover duration
The number of databases in a failover group "directly affects the duration of both Failover and Forced Failover operations": a planned failover synchronizes all primary databases and prepares them in batches, so Learn recommends limiting the number of databases in a failover group, and for a forced failover a smaller number gives quicker and more predictable durations. Several failover groups can be created between the same two servers, and each fails over independently.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#number-of-databases-in-failover-group

### The listener endpoints follow the primary and a DNS record has a 30-second TTL
The read-write listener is the DNS CNAME record fog-name.database.windows.net and the read-only listener is fog-name.secondary.database.windows.net; they do not change after a failover. The failover updates the DNS record, so client connections are redirected only after the client DNS cache is refreshed, and the time to live of both listener records is 30 seconds. By default, failover of the read-only listener is disabled, so read-only sessions cannot connect while the secondary is offline. Read-only sessions use ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly in the connection string.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-sql-db?view=azuresql#use-the-read-write-listener-primary

### Active geo-replication has no stable endpoint
After either failover or forced failover of active geo-replication, "the connection endpoint for the new primary changes because the new primary is now located on a different logical server". The application, a DNS entry or a connection string setting must be changed to reach the new primary, which is the reason Learn points to failover groups when a stable connection endpoint is needed.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#terminology-and-capabilities

### Failover without data loss and forced failover
A failover (no data loss) performs a full data synchronization before the roles switch, so it is possible only when the primary is accessible, and it is used for DR drills in production, for relocating a database and for failing back. A forced failover immediately switches the secondary to the primary role without waiting for the changes to propagate, so transactions committed on the primary but not yet replicated are lost; it is the recovery method when the primary is not accessible. When the original primary is back online, it is reconnected automatically and, in active geo-replication, reseeded and made the new geo-secondary.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#terminology-and-capabilities

### Asynchronous replication makes data loss unavoidable unless the application waits
"Asynchronous replication makes the possibility of data loss unavoidable if the primary fails." For critical transactions, the application can call sp_wait_for_database_copy_sync right after a commit, which blocks the calling thread until the last committed transaction has been transmitted and hardened in the transaction log of the secondary; it is scoped to one geo-replication link, does not guarantee full synchronization for read access, and its delay can be significant.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#prevent-loss-of-critical-data

### Waiting for the secondary needs custom development and costs performance
In the reliability article's considerations, Learn says that to eliminate data loss from asynchronous replication during failovers, the application can be configured to block the calling thread until the last committed transaction is transmitted and hardened in the transaction log of the secondary database, and that "This approach requires custom development and reduces the performance of your application."
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#considerations

### A database in a failover group is failed over with the group
If a database is a member of a failover group, it cannot be failed over with the geo-replication failover command: the failover command of the group is used. To fail over an individual database it must first be removed from the failover group.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#prepare-for-geo-failover

### The backup retention policy is not replicated to the geo-secondary
The backup retention policy "isn't a part of the database and isn't replicated from the primary". By default the geo-secondary has a point-in-time restore retention period of seven days, so the retention of the secondary is set to match the primary's before a failover.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#prepare-for-geo-failover

### Logins must exist on the target server
Users that are mapped to logins in the master database need the same logins on the target server before the failover or the geo-restore; "if you fail over or geo-restore to a server that does not have properly configured logins, access to it will be limited to the server admin account". Learn's three steps are to determine the logins with access to the primary database, find their SIDs, and create the logins with the same SIDs on the target server. A contained database user is managed by the database itself, so it keeps working on the new primary or on a geo-restored database without extra configuration, at the cost of managing credentials per database.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-security-configure?view=azuresql

### Database-level firewall rules travel with the database
Learn recommends database-level IP firewall rules for geo-replicated databases because "these rules are replicated with the database", so every geo-secondary has the same IP firewall rules as the primary, and contained users so that credentials are the same on the primary and the secondary. This means server-level firewall rules and logins are not carried by that statement and are created on the DR server before the failover.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#keep-credentials-and-firewall-rules-in-sync

### Adding a geo-secondary by T-SQL is not supported over a private endpoint
"Adding a geo-secondary using T-SQL is not supported when connecting to the primary server over a private endpoint." If a private endpoint is configured but public network access is allowed, adding a geo-secondary is supported from a public IP address, and once the geo-secondary is added public network access can be denied. With a network security perimeter (preview) on a logical server, Transact-SQL is not a supported way to add a geo-secondary either, rules are needed in both directions between every pair of servers of the geo-replication set, and chaining is not supported.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#private-endpoints

### Active geo-replication across subscriptions
The Azure portal can set up active geo-replication across subscriptions when both are in the same Microsoft Entra tenant. For a different tenant, SQL authentication and T-SQL are needed, and Microsoft Entra authentication for cross-subscription geo-replication is not supported when the logical server is in a different tenant. Up to four geo-secondaries can be created for a primary, and a secondary of a secondary (chaining) is supported only programmatically.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-overview?view=azuresql#cross-subscription-geo-replication

### Roles to create and fail over a failover group
Permissions for a failover group are managed by Azure role-based access control: Azure RBAC write access is needed to create and manage failover groups, and the SQL Server Contributor role has all the necessary permissions. Creating a failover group needs write access on the primary server, the secondary server and all databases in the group; failing over needs write access on the failover group on the new server.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/failover-group-configure-sql-db?view=azuresql#permissions

### Role to add a geo-secondary
Learn's portal tutorial says that to add a secondary database "you must be the subscription owner or co-owner". If the partner database already exists, for example as a result of terminating a previous geo-replication relationship, the command fails. Learn's failover steps then switch the secondary to the primary role, and "Remove secondary database" permanently stops replication and makes the secondary a regular read-write database.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/active-geo-replication-configure-portal?view=azuresql

### Roles to geo-restore
To recover a database by using automated backups, geo-restore included, you must be a member of the Contributor role or the SQL Server Contributor role in the subscription or resource group that contains the logical server, or the subscription or resource group owner.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql

### A standby replica saves licensing cost with limits
A secondary used only for DR can be designated as a standby replica, which is licensed free of the vCores of the primary, while compute and storage are still billed. It is supported for single databases in the provisioned vCore model in General Purpose or Business Critical on standard-series (Gen5) or DC-series hardware; it is not supported for serverless, elastic pools, the DTU model or Hyperscale. Only one secondary replica can be standby, and in a failover group the setting is per database and, in the failover group creation and add steps, available only in the Azure portal. A standby replica allows only DR activities: monitoring, maintenance such as DBCC CHECKDB and DR drills.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/standby-replica-how-to-configure?view=azuresql

### Detection of a regional outage is the customer's job
"Microsoft doesn't automatically notify you when a region is down." Azure Service Health shows the overall health of the service, including region failures, and Service Health alerts notify of problems. With active geo-replication, and with a failover group on the customer-managed policy, the customer is responsible for detecting the outage and for triggering the failover.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#failover-groups

### Failback is always started by the customer
After the primary region recovers, the customer can fail back by performing another failover; for a failover group, "even if Microsoft initiated the original failover, you're still responsible for failing back to the previous region, if you choose to". Learn names no automatic failback.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#failover-groups

### Geo-restore needs geo-redundant backup storage
Geo-restore is available only for databases configured with geo-redundant or geo-zone-redundant (GZRS) backup storage. It is available when the PITR backup storage is geo-redundant or GZRS and is not available when it is zone-redundant or locally redundant. All PITR backups are geo-redundant by default, so geo-restore is enabled by default.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore

### Geo-restore is not available in regions with no region pair
In its disaster recovery checklist, Learn says that setting the backup storage redundancy to geo-redundant backup storage to use geo-restore "isn't available in regions with no region pair". So a primary region with no pair has no geo-restore from geo-redundant backups, and the options for it are replication to a DR region (failover group or active geo-replication) or the export described in the constraint on backups outside the pair.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/high-availability-disaster-recovery-checklist?view=azuresql#disaster-recovery-checklist

### Geo-restore targets are stated differently by two Learn pages
The reliability article says the geo-redundant copy of the backups "enables geo-restore of your backups into the paired region". The restore article says "You can restore a database on any logical server in any Azure region from the most recent geo-replicated backups" and "Geo-restore is the default recovery option when your database is unavailable because of an incident in the hosting region". The backups are stored in the pair, and Learn's restore article lets the new database be created in any region, so a DR region that is not the pair is a documented target; because the two pages are worded differently, the first geo-restore drill into the chosen non-pair region (TC-8) is what proves it for a given subscription.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore

### Geo-restore works only in the same subscription
"You can perform geo-restore only on databases that reside in the same subscription." So the DR server that receives a geo-restore is in the subscription of the databases.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore

### Restoring into another subscription is not supported
Restoring a new database in another subscription is "Not supported" for geo-restore and for restore from long-term retention backups; Learn's workaround is to restore to a new server and use Resource Move to move the server to another subscription, or to use a cross-subscription database copy.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/automated-backups-overview?view=azuresql

### Geo-restore does not guarantee capacity in the target region
Geo-restore "doesn't guarantee that the target region will have the capacity to restore your databases after a regional outage, because a sharp increase of demand is likely". Learn says it is an appropriate disaster-recovery solution when the application uses relatively small databases and is not critical to the business, and that business-critical applications with large databases use failover groups, which offer a much lower RPO and RTO and always guarantee the capacity.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore-considerations

### Restore requests are limited per subscription
For a single subscription, at most 30 requests for single databases are processed concurrently and 100 can be submitted; for an elastic pool, 4 are processed concurrently per pool and 2,000 can be submitted. The limits apply to any combination of point-in-time restores, geo-restores and restores from long-term retention backups. Recovery time depends on the size of the database, its compute size, the size of the transaction log backups, the activity to replay, the network bandwidth to a different region and the number of concurrent restore requests in the target region.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql

### Changing the backup storage redundancy has effects on geo-restore
Changes to the backup storage redundancy of an existing database apply to future backups only, "it might take up to 48 hours for the changes to be applied on the database", and switching from geo-redundant backup storage to locally redundant or zone-redundant storage disables geo-restore. Learn says that "Except for Hyperscale and Basic databases, you can update the backup storage redundancy setting for an existing database". For Hyperscale databases the redundancy can be set only when the database is created, and Learn's ways to change it later are active geo-replication, database copy or point-in-time restore; the page names no in-place change for a Basic database.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/automated-backups-change-settings?view=azuresql#configure-backup-storage-redundancy

### A restore into a region without the source's backup redundancy fails
When the source backup storage redundancy is GZRS, a new database made by any restore, geo-restore included, inherits the source's configuration unless the redundancy is specified; if the target region does not support that redundancy, the restore "fails with an appropriate error message", which is mitigated by specifying an available storage option for the region. An Azure Policy such as "Azure SQL DB should avoid using GRS backup" can also make a database creation with geo-redundant backup storage fail.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/automated-backups-overview?view=azuresql

### Published RTO and RPO figures of the options
Learn's business continuity table gives, for disaster recovery with failover groups on the customer-managed policy or active geo-replication, an RTO of "Typically less than 60 seconds" and an RPO of "Equal to or greater than 0" that depends on the data changes not yet replicated; for geo-restore it gives an RTO and an RPO of "Typically minutes or hours", the RTO "dependent on Azure storage replication" and the RPO "dependent on size of database backup". These are typical values and not guarantees.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo

### Backups outside the pair need an export
In the reliability article, Learn says that for a nonpaired region, or when backups must be replicated to a region other than the paired region, "consider exporting the database and storing the exported file in a storage account that uses blob object replication to replicate to a storage account in another region". This is a copy of the data at the time of the export, not a continuous replication.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#backup-storage

### A recovered database needs its surroundings configured
After a geo-failover or a geo-restore, Learn's checklist is: update connection strings (not needed for failover group listener endpoints), make the firewall rules on the secondary server and database match the primary's, create the logins that must be present in the master database of the new primary, update the alert rules to map to the new primary database and server, and make the auditing configuration identical on the secondary server.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/disaster-recovery-guidance?view=azuresql#configure-your-database-after-recovery

### Drills: a planned failover is safe in production and a geo-restore drill uses a copy
A planned failover of a failover group keeps the primary and secondary in sync when the roles are switched and does not lose data, so the drill can be performed in the production environment. A geo-restore drill is done on a test environment that is a copy of production, because it can lose data; Learn's way to simulate the outage is to rename the source database, and its validation covers connection strings, logins and basic functionality. Learn recommends periodic drills to verify the application's tolerance to the loss of recent data and the operational aspects of the recovery.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/disaster-recovery-drills?view=azuresql

## Non-paired options

### failover-group: Failover group to a logical server in the DR region
- Kind: native
- Target: dr-region
- Uses mechanism: Failover groups
- Estimated RPO: 5m (Estimated, team-set starting value for the replication-lag bound; TC-2 proves it. Learn gives no figure: its business continuity table states an RPO of "Equal to or greater than 0" that depends on the data changes not yet replicated, and replication is asynchronous, so the value is never 0 — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo)
- Estimated RTO: 60s (Learn: "Typically less than 60 seconds" of downtime for disaster recovery with failover groups on the customer-managed failover policy — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo; it is the database failover only, the application's own start in the DR region is not in it, so TC-3 measures the whole switch-over)
- Prerequisites: a primary and a secondary logical server in the same Microsoft Entra tenant, in different regions, the secondary in any region (paired regions are recommended, not required); the same service tier, compute tier, compute size, backup storage redundancy and firewall rules on the secondary databases; a DR server whose login and firewall settings match the primary server's and whose logins have the same SIDs (or contained users); no database with the same name on the secondary server, and an elastic pool of the same name with enough free capacity when the primary is in a pool; the DR region's support for availability zones checked when the primary is zone redundant (error 45122), and zone redundancy of the secondaries enabled after the group is created for databases other than Hyperscale; a maintenance window on the DR server that differs from the primary's; a private endpoint and a private DNS zone for each server and virtual networks with nonoverlapping address spaces when private access is used, proved by TC-6 because Learn documents the procedure for paired regions; no virtual network service endpoint rules if the Microsoft-managed policy is wanted (the customer-managed policy is the recommended one and is what this option assumes); the SQL Server Contributor role (or Azure RBAC write access) on both servers and all databases for the identity that creates and fails over the group; the application connects through the read-write listener fog-name.database.windows.net and handles transient faults with retry logic; the DR region's front-end components exist, because latency between dependent components rises after a failover that moves only the databases.
- Cost notes: the secondary databases are billed as separate databases; a standby replica can reduce the cost when the secondary has no read or write workload (limits in the constraint on standby replicas); no price.
- Gives up: no zero data loss on a forced failover (replication is asynchronous, so the changes not yet replicated are lost); a Microsoft-managed policy responds only after a grace period of at least an hour and, per Learn, likely several hours at least, so the customer-managed policy and the customer's own detection are used; one secondary server per group (multiple secondaries are a preview that is not recommended for production); for databases other than Hyperscale the secondary databases are not zone redundant unless enabled afterwards; Learn's private link procedure covers paired regions only.
- Recommended when: the DR region is not the primary's pair or is any other region, several databases of one application fail over together, the application needs connection endpoints that do not change, and a recovery time of minutes is needed.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#failover-groups

### active-geo-replication: Active geo-replication of each database to a server in the DR region
- Kind: native
- Target: dr-region
- Uses mechanism: Active geo-replication
- Estimated RPO: 5m (Estimated, team-set starting value for the replication-lag bound, read from replication_lag_sec; TC-2 proves it. Learn gives no figure: its business continuity table states an RPO of "Equal to or greater than 0" that depends on the data changes not yet replicated, and replication is asynchronous, so the value is never 0 — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo)
- Estimated RTO: 60s (Learn: "Typically less than 60 seconds" of downtime for disaster recovery with active geo-replication — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo; the time to reconfigure the application to connect to the new primary server is added to it, because the connection endpoint changes, so TC-3 measures the whole switch-over)
- Prerequisites: a logical server in the DR region, any region (paired regions are recommended, not required), other than the primary's server; the same service tier as the primary and the same compute tier, compute size and backup storage redundancy as recommended; the same server-level firewall rules and the same logins (SIDs) on the DR server, database-level firewall rules and contained users when used; the backup retention of the geo-secondary set to match the primary's (the default is seven days); a maintenance window on the DR server that differs from the primary's; the role of subscription owner or co-owner to add a secondary in the portal (cross-subscription geo-replication needs the same Microsoft Entra tenant for the portal, else SQL authentication and T-SQL); the portal, Azure CLI or PowerShell instead of T-SQL when the primary is reached over a private endpoint or sits in a network security perimeter; an application whose connection string or DNS alias for the database can be changed to the DR server without a code change, with retry logic; no failover through the geo-replication command for a database that is in a failover group.
- Cost notes: each secondary database is billed as a separate database; a standby replica can reduce the cost when the secondary has no read or write workload (limits in the constraint on standby replicas); no price.
- Gives up: no stable listener endpoint (the application, a DNS entry or the connection string has to be switched to the new primary server); no failover of a group of databases as one unit; asynchronous replication, so a forced failover loses the changes not yet replicated; the failover is customer-initiated and the detection of the regional outage is the customer's job; the secondary does not carry the backup retention policy or the server-level logins and firewall rules.
- Recommended when: a single database or a few independent databases need a DR copy in the DR region, a stable listener endpoint is not needed or an alias or DNS record already abstracts the server name, more than one secondary is wanted, or the databases are in different subscriptions or a zone-redundancy setting differs per secondary.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/reliability/reliability-sql-database#active-geo-replication

### geo-restore: Geo-restore from geo-redundant backups into a server in the DR region
- Kind: backup-restore
- Target: dr-region
- Uses mechanism: Geo-restore
- Estimated RPO: 1h (Estimated, team-set starting value; TC-8 proves it. Learn: recovery "to the point of the most recent replicated backups", the replication of Geo-Redundant Storage is "in the range of minutes" without a specific time frame, and its business continuity table gives "Typically minutes or hours" — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo)
- Estimated RTO: 12h (Estimated, team-set starting value; TC-8 proves it. Learn: the restore "might take several hours" for a large or very active database outside Hyperscale, its business continuity table gives "Typically minutes or hours", it depends on the size, compute size, log backups, bandwidth to the other region and the number of concurrent requests, and has no capacity guarantee — https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/business-continuity-high-availability-disaster-recover-hadr-overview?view=azuresql#rto-and-rpo)
- Prerequisites: geo-redundant backup storage (or GZRS) on every database in scope, checked per database because it is the default and can be changed (a change takes up to 48 hours and applies to future backups; Hyperscale and Basic databases cannot be updated in place); a primary region that has a region pair (geo-restore from geo-redundant backups is not available in regions with no region pair); a prepared logical server in the DR region, in the same subscription as the databases, with the firewall rules, the logins with the same SIDs (or contained users), the auditing configuration and the alert rules that the recovered databases need; the Contributor or SQL Server Contributor role (or owner) on the subscription or resource group; a DR region that supports the backup storage redundancy that the new database inherits (GZRS) or an explicit redundancy for the restore; a runbook that orders the restores by priority against the limit of 30 concurrently processed requests per subscription; the application's connection string held as configuration so that it can point at the new server; for a primary region with no pair, the export of the database and blob object replication of the exported file to a storage account in another region described in Learn is the alternative to prepare, and it is a copy at the time of the export.
- Cost notes: no second database runs before the disaster, so there is no standing compute cost; the geo-redundant backup storage (the default); the restored databases are billed from the restore; no price.
- Gives up: the lowest cost and the weakest recovery figures: an Estimated RPO that is only a team-set starting value and an Estimated RTO of several hours for large or very active databases; no capacity guarantee in the target region after a regional outage; no recovery of the changes after the most recent replicated backup; the backups are stored in the pair region of the primary, which matters for data residency; a new server name, so the application must be reconfigured; the primary must have a region pair.
- Recommended when: the databases are relatively small and not business-critical, the cost of a standing replica is not justified and a recovery time of hours and a loss of recent data are accepted.
- Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-sql/database/recovery-using-backups?view=azuresql#geo-restore

## Migration from a paired setup

### Recognise today's setup
A database whose DR is the primary region's pair shows in an inventory in one or more of three ways. First, a failover group: a failover group resource of the primary logical server whose partner server is in the pair region; its failover policy value is manual for the customer-managed policy and automatic for the Microsoft-managed policy. Second, a geo-replication link: a replication link on the primary database whose partner server is in the pair region (the replication-link and partner-server properties are the evidence); a replica type of Standby on the secondary means it is a license-free standby replica. A database of the same name on a server in the pair region, or a server's region alone, never proves a link: without the replication link, record the relationship as Unknown. Third, no replica at all: the backup storage redundancy of the database is geo-redundant or geo-zone-redundant (the default), so the automated backups are copied to the paired region and geo-restore is available; this is the implicit paired DR of every database that has no replica. The inventory cannot show the replication lag, whether the secondary matches the primary's service tier, compute size and backup retention, whether the DR server holds the logins with the same SIDs and the firewall rules, whether the application uses the listener endpoints or a server name, whether a failover or a geo-restore was ever rehearsed, or whether the pair region can be used by the company's data residency rules, so record these as Unknown until a test shows them. A database can have a replica and geo-redundant backups together: record the replica as today's DR and the geo-redundant backups as its fallback. Migration to a DR region that is not the pair is decided per database or per failover group by option id: failover-group (databases of one application and a stable endpoint), active-geo-replication (individual databases, several secondaries, or per-database control) or geo-restore (only where a standing replica is not justified); the options can be mixed across the databases of one application, never on one database.

### Build
1. Take the inventory per database: the logical server, service tier, purchasing model, compute tier (provisioned or serverless), compute size, hardware, elastic pool, Hyperscale or not, zone redundancy, backup storage redundancy and PITR retention, failover group membership and policy, replication links and standby type, the application's connection string style (listener, server name or alias), the logins (names and SIDs) and contained users, the server-level and database-level firewall rules, the private endpoints and DNS, the auditing configuration, the maintenance window and the alert rules; this inventory is what TC-1 and TC-6 compare.
2. Choose the DR region and check it for each database: the service tier and hardware are offered there, availability zones are supported there when the primary is zone redundant (the failover group fails with error 45122 otherwise), the subscription's quota allows the DR servers and databases, and the data residency rules accept the region; choose the option per database or per group from the inventory.
3. failover-group: create the DR logical server in the DR region in the same Microsoft Entra tenant, with its Microsoft Entra administrator, the same server-level firewall rules as the primary server, the logins with the same SIDs as on the primary server (do not create a login with the primary's server admin SID: make the DR server's admin login a database principal instead), the same auditing configuration, a maintenance window different from the primary's, and a private endpoint, a private DNS zone link and a virtual network with an address space that does not overlap the primary's when the primary uses private access; assign the SQL Server Contributor role to the identity that builds and fails over the failover group.
4. active-geo-replication: create the DR logical server in the DR region in the same Microsoft Entra tenant, with its Microsoft Entra administrator, the same server-level firewall rules as the primary server, the logins with the same SIDs as on the primary server (do not create a login with the primary's server admin SID: make the DR server's admin login a database principal instead), the same auditing configuration, a maintenance window different from the primary's, and a private endpoint, a private DNS zone link and a virtual network with an address space that does not overlap the primary's when the primary uses private access; assign the owner role (needed to add a geo-secondary in the portal) to the identity that builds and fails over.
5. failover-group: when a failover group already exists to the pair, keep it running and follow Learn's "change the secondary region" sequence in the next steps: with server A as the primary, server B as the secondary in the pair and server C as the new DR server, create an additional secondary of each database of server A on server C with active geo-replication (each database then has two secondaries), so that the primary databases stay protected during the transition; the group between A and C does not exist until the cut-over, so until then server C holds plain geo-secondaries of databases that are members of the group A-B; enable zone redundancy on the new secondaries where the primary has it, because it is not set by the failover group.
6. failover-group: when no failover group exists to the pair, create the failover group on the primary server to server C with the customer-managed failover policy (manual), add the databases (a secondary is created on server C with the same edition and compute size; an elastic pool of the same name with free capacity must exist on server C), add standby rights per database in the portal when the secondary is DR-only, enable zone redundancy on the secondaries afterwards (not Hyperscale), and wait for the initial seeding (up to 500 GB an hour, in parallel for all databases); the application connects through fog-name.database.windows.net with ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly for read-only sessions on fog-name.secondary.database.windows.net.
7. active-geo-replication: create a geo-secondary of each database on the DR server (the same service tier and, as recommended, the same compute tier, compute size and backup storage redundancy; with the portal, Azure CLI or PowerShell when private endpoints or a network security perimeter are used), set its PITR retention to the primary's, optionally designate it as standby, and keep the existing secondary in the pair until the cut-over (up to four secondaries are supported); a database that is a member of a failover group is not failed over with this command, so databases of a group stay in the group.
8. geo-restore: set the backup storage redundancy of every database in scope to geo-redundant or GZRS (it applies to future backups and can take up to 48 hours; check that no Azure Policy such as "Azure SQL DB should avoid using GRS backup" blocks it); a Basic or Hyperscale database cannot be updated in place (for Hyperscale Learn's ways are active geo-replication, database copy or point-in-time restore; Learn names none for Basic), so a Basic or Hyperscale database whose redundancy is not geo-redundant uses failover-group or active-geo-replication, or is re-created with geo-redundant backup storage; create the DR logical server in the DR region, in the subscription of the databases and the same Microsoft Entra tenant, with its Microsoft Entra administrator and, when the primary uses private access, a private endpoint, a private DNS zone link and a virtual network with an address space that does not overlap the primary's (its logins, firewall rules and auditing are prepared in the next step); assign the Contributor or SQL Server Contributor role on the subscription or resource group of the DR server to the identity that restores, and the subscription owner or co-owner role to the identity that adds the geo-secondary in the primary region during failback (adding a geo-secondary in the portal); write the runbook that lists the databases by priority, the target server and region, the restore commands and the post-restore configuration of the next step; set the application to take its database server name from configuration.
9. geo-restore: prepare the post-restore configuration on the DR server before DR, at any time while the original server is online: the logins with their SIDs, the server-level firewall rules, the auditing configuration, the alert rules for the new database and server, and the connection string change; Learn says access is limited to the server admin account if the logins are not in place.
10. Set the alerts: an Azure Service Health alert on the primary region and the availability metric of the databases; update the alert rules of the DR server so that they map to the new primary after a failover.
11. failover-group: add an alert when replication_lag_sec exceeds the team's lag bound or stays NULL for an extended period.
12. active-geo-replication: add an alert when replication_lag_sec exceeds the team's lag bound or stays NULL for an extended period.
13. Configure the application: retry logic for transient faults, the connection string or listener name taken from configuration so that the DR deployment can use the DR server without a code change, and, where the application must not lose a critical transaction, a call to sp_wait_for_database_copy_sync after the commit with its performance cost accepted by the application owner.

### Validate
1. active-geo-replication: run TC-1 on the DR server and its databases once, then weekly, and TC-2 for 7 consecutive days.
2. failover-group: run TC-1 on the DR server and its databases once, then weekly, and TC-2 for 7 consecutive days; for the existing-group path the failover group clause of TC-1 cannot be met before the cut-over (the group to the DR server does not exist yet) and is run in the cut-over step that checks the listeners of the re-created group.
3. Run TC-6 from the DR region's network and with every kind of principal that the application uses.
4. failover-group: where the failover group to the DR server is new (the Build step that creates the failover group to server C when no group exists to the pair), run TC-5 (failover policy and the use of the listener endpoints) and keep the group on the customer-managed policy; for an existing group to the pair (the Build step that keeps the group to the pair running and adds a geo-secondary on server C) the group to server C does not exist yet, so TC-5 and TC-3 run on it in the cut-over step that checks the listeners of the re-created group, and the pair's secondaries stay until they pass.
5. failover-group: run TC-3 as a planned failover (no data loss) drill in production for each new failover group (the Build step that creates the failover group to server C when no group exists to the pair), time it, and fail back as in the Failback table; for the existing-group path TC-3 runs on the new group in the cut-over step that checks the listeners of the re-created group.
6. active-geo-replication: run TC-3 as a planned failover (no data loss) drill in production for a sample of databases in active geo-replication that are not members of a failover group, time it, and fail back as in the Failback table; a database that is a member of the group to the pair cannot be failed over with the geo-replication command.
7. failover-group: run TC-4 in a test environment that is a copy of production (a forced failover can lose data).
8. active-geo-replication: run TC-4 in a test environment that is a copy of production (a forced failover can lose data).
9. geo-restore: run TC-7 (backup storage redundancy), then TC-8 (a geo-restore drill into the DR region on a test copy, timed, with the age of the restore point recorded) and TC-9 (the restore request limits and the capacity design check, which cannot be drilled).
10. Record each result with its date; a time or figure that a drill measured is Demonstrated, and everything else, including the recovery time of a Microsoft-managed failover and the capacity of the DR region after a real outage, stays Estimated or Unknown.

### Cut over
1. failover-group: after the validation passed, delete the failover group between server A and server B (Learn: "at this point sign in attempts using failover group endpoints start to fail", because the listener DNS records are deleted, so do it in an agreed window and have the application use the servers' own names for that time), then create the failover group again with the same name between server A and server C and add all the primary databases of server A to it ("at this point sign in attempts stop failing").
2. failover-group: check that the read-write listener fog-name.database.windows.net resolves to the primary server, that the read-only listener resolves to server C, and that the application connects; the sign-in failures end only after the client DNS caches are refreshed (the time to live is 30 seconds). For the existing-group path, run TC-5 and the planned failover drill TC-3 (with its failback), and the failover group clause of TC-1, on the new group now, while the old secondaries on server B still exist; the databases on server B remain after the old group is deleted, because removing a failover group does not stop replication.
3. active-geo-replication: after the validation passed, change the DR runbook and the DR deployment's configuration so that the DR server in the DR region is the failover target, and make the DR server's alerts and the application's DR switch-over procedure the ones of TC-3.
4. geo-restore: change the runbook and the DR deployment's configuration so that the DR server in the DR region is the restore target, and remove the runbook steps that restore into a server in the pair.
5. Put the Failover steps of the option in the runbook, with the incident manager's decision rule (Learn: disaster recovery should be performed only when the outage duration approaches the application's recovery time objective) and the lag or restore-point loss that the application owner accepts.

### Decommission
1. failover-group: after the failover group to server C has been validated (for the existing-group path, after TC-5 and TC-3 passed on the new group in the cut-over step that checks the listeners of the re-created group) and has run, delete server B in the pair region, but only when it holds nothing but the old secondary databases: Learn's last step is "Delete server B. All databases on B will be deleted automatically".
2. active-geo-replication: after the validation passed, stop replication to the secondary in the pair region (the secondary becomes a regular read-write database that is not part of any replication), then delete that database and, when it holds nothing else, its server; do not delete a secondary of a failover group before removing it from the group.
3. geo-restore: nothing is decommissioned; the geo-redundant backups keep being copied to the pair region by design. If the company's data residency rules do not accept backup copies in the pair, raise it as a residency decision: switching to locally or zone-redundant backup storage disables geo-restore, and Learn's alternative is the export with blob object replication.

### Keep until cut-over
The replication to the pair region (the failover group, the geo-replication link and the secondary databases) stays, with the pair's server, its logins, firewall rules, private endpoint and the runbook steps of the paired failover, until TC-1, TC-2 and TC-6 have passed for the DR region (for the existing-group path the failover group clause of TC-1 is checked after the group to the DR server was re-created in the cut-over, and the rest of TC-1 before the cut-over) and, for failover-group, TC-3 and TC-5 have passed too, and for active-geo-replication TC-3 has passed (TC-5 applies to failover-group only): for a new group or for active geo-replication that is before the cut-over, and for the existing-group path it is after the group to the DR server was re-created in the cut-over, so the pair's secondaries outlive the cut-over until then. For geo-restore it stays until TC-6, TC-7, TC-8 and TC-9 have passed. The geo-redundant backup storage stays for good, for geo-restore and as the fallback of the replicas.

## Test criteria

### TC-1: The secondary matches the primary
- Applies to: failover-group, active-geo-replication
- Pass condition: for every database in scope, a secondary exists on the DR server and its replication link is healthy; its service tier equals the primary's, its compute tier, compute size, backup storage redundancy and PITR retention equal the primary's or differ by an exception that the application owner has written down; its zone redundancy is as the plan says; the DR server holds every login of the inventory with the same SID and every server-level firewall rule of the primary server; and for a failover group that exists to the DR server (a new group, or the group of the existing-group path once the cut-over re-created it), the failover policy is manual (customer managed) and the group holds exactly the databases of the inventory.
- How to measure: read the database, replication link and failover group properties (az sql db replica list-links and az sql failover-group show, or Get-AzSqlDatabaseReplicationLink and Get-AzSqlDatabaseFailoverGroup) and compare them with the inventory; compare the SQL logins and their SIDs in the master databases of both servers and the server-level firewall rules with read-only queries.
- Evidence: the comparison output with its date: per database the properties of both sides and the differences with their exceptions; per server the login names and SIDs and the firewall rule list, never a password or a secret.
- Frequency: once before cut-over (the failover group clause of the existing-group path after the group is re-created), then weekly and after every change to a database's tier, size or redundancy

### TC-2: The replication lag stays below the lag bound
- Applies to: failover-group, active-geo-replication
- Pass condition: for 7 consecutive days, replication_lag_sec of every replication link stays at or below the replication-lag bound (the Estimated RPO of the option: the team-set starting value of 5m, or a lower bound that the application owner sets from the application's tolerance for data loss and writes in the runbook), and a value of NULL (the primary does not know how far behind the geo-secondary is) never lasts longer than the period that the team sets with its alert.
- How to measure: query the replication_lag_sec column of sys.dm_geo_replication_link_status on each primary database at a fixed interval and store the values; compare the last_commit time on the geo-secondary with the same value on the primary; an alert fires when the bound is exceeded or the value is NULL for the period.
- Evidence: the 7-day export of the lag per database with its maximum, the bound it was compared with, and the alert rule and its test.
- Frequency: continuous collection with an alert; reviewed before cut-over and then monthly

### TC-3: A planned failover meets the recovery time
- Applies to: failover-group, active-geo-replication
- Pass condition: in a failover (no data loss) drill, the database role switch completes within the failover-duration bound that the team sets in the runbook and records with the size of the group (Learn: "typically up to 60 seconds of downtime" for a customer-initiated failover, and "several minutes" for a planned geo-failover of a group, depending on its size; the first drill sets the bound, and 60s is the figure to compare it with), no committed transaction is lost, and the application's first successful write through the failover group listener (failover-group) or through the new primary server after its switch-over (active-geo-replication) comes within the switch-over time that the team sets in the runbook; the failback of the Failback table is run in the same drill.
- How to measure: a drill that starts the failover of the group or database (az sql failover-group set-primary or Switch-AzSqlDatabaseFailoverGroup; az sql db replica set-primary or Set-AzSqlDatabaseSecondary) and times it from the start to the new primary role, then to the first successful write of a probe that writes a row with a timestamp every second; the probe also counts rows to prove that none was lost.
- Evidence: the drill record with its timestamps, the probe log, the roles of the servers before and after, the measured times and the failback times.
- Frequency: before the pair's secondaries are decommissioned (for the existing-group path, after the group to the DR server was re-created), then at the interval the team sets in the runbook (Learn recommends periodic drills), and after every change to how the application connects

### TC-4: A forced failover loses no more than the lag bound
- Applies to: failover-group, active-geo-replication
- Pass condition: in a test environment that copies production, a forced failover completes, the new primary accepts writes, the number of transactions or the time of the data lost (last commit on the old primary compared with the last commit on the new primary) is within the replication-lag bound of TC-2, and the old primary reconnects as the secondary when it is back.
- How to measure: a drill in a test environment that runs a probe writing a timestamped row every second, forces the failover (Azure CLI with --allow-data-loss, PowerShell with -AllowDataLoss, or Forced Failover in the portal), compares the last row on both sides, and checks the replication link of the old primary after it returns.
- Evidence: the drill record, the last timestamp on each side, the data loss measured and the state of the link after the return.
- Frequency: before cut-over, then yearly or at the interval the team sets, and after a change of the lag bound

### TC-5: Failover policy and the application's use of the listener
- Applies to: failover-group
- Pass condition: every failover group of the workload has its failover policy set to manual (customer managed) unless a written exception by the application owner exists for the Microsoft-managed policy, and then the group has no virtual network rules on its servers; the application's configuration for the database holds the read-write listener name fog-name.database.windows.net (and the read-only listener with ApplicationIntent=ReadOnly where it uses one) and no server name; both listener names resolve to the expected servers from the DR region's network.
- How to measure: read the failover policy of each group in the failover group properties, search the application's configuration for server names, and resolve the two listener names from inside the primary and the DR networks.
- Evidence: the failover policy of each group, the configuration search result and the resolved addresses with their date.
- Frequency: before cut-over for a new group, in the cut-over step that checks the listeners of the re-created group for the existing-group path, then monthly and after every change to the application's database configuration

### TC-6: The DR server is reachable and the principals sign in
- Applies to: failover-group, active-geo-replication, geo-restore
- Pass condition: from the DR region's network, the DR server name resolves to the address the design says (the private IP address of the private endpoint when private access is used), a sign-in with every kind of principal that the application uses (SQL login, contained user, Microsoft Entra principal) succeeds on the DR server and on a database that holds the replica or a restored test database, the server-level firewall rules and the auditing configuration equal the primary server's, and for private access the check passes in the DR region's network with the DR server's private endpoint and DNS zone link; for a failover group with private access this check is the proof of the path that Learn documents only for paired regions.
- How to measure: resolve the server name from a host in the DR network, run a connection test for each principal against the DR server, compare the server-level firewall rules of the two servers (portal, Azure CLI or PowerShell) and the database-level firewall rules of the databases with Transact-SQL (Learn: database-level IP firewall rules can only be configured through Transact-SQL, and they are replicated with the database), and compare the auditing configuration of the two servers (the auditing policy of each server, read in the portal or with the Azure CLI), because Learn says to make the auditing configuration identical on the secondary server.
- Evidence: the resolved addresses, the sign-in results per principal type, and the firewall and auditing comparison with their date.
- Frequency: before cut-over, then monthly and after every change to the DR network, DNS, firewall rules, logins or auditing

### TC-7: Every database has geo-redundant backup storage
- Applies to: geo-restore
- Pass condition: every database in scope has the backup storage redundancy geo-redundant or geo-zone-redundant, no Azure Policy denies it, the primary region has a region pair, and the backups of each database include restore points no older than the PITR retention that the team sets.
- How to measure: read the backup storage redundancy of each database (az sql db show, Get-AzSqlDatabase) and the list of geo-restore backups (Get-AzSqlDatabaseGeoBackup), read the policy assignments of the subscription and the region's pair from the Learn pairing list.
- Evidence: the list of databases with their redundancy and the date of the newest restore point, the policy check result and the region pair record.
- Frequency: monthly and after every database creation or redundancy change

### TC-8: A geo-restore drill into the DR region meets the recovery figures
- Applies to: geo-restore
- Pass condition: in a drill on a test copy of production, a geo-restore of each database in scope into the DR server in the DR region (the non-pair region when that is the DR region) completes, the restored database is Online, the sentinel data check passes up to the restore point, the restore point is no older than the Estimated RPO of the option (the team-set starting value of 1h, or a bound that the application owner accepts and records), the restore completes within the Estimated RTO of the option (the team-set starting value of 12h, or a time that the application owner accepts and records), and the post-restore configuration of the Failover table lets every kind of principal sign in.
- How to measure: simulate the outage by renaming the source database in the test copy, geo-restore with the portal, Azure CLI or PowerShell (Get-AzSqlDatabaseGeoBackup and Restore-AzSqlDatabase) into the DR server, time it from the request to the Online state, read the restore point, and run the application's smoke test and sentinel data check.
- Evidence: the drill record with the timestamps, the restore point and its age, the measured restore duration per database and its size, and the smoke test log.
- Frequency: before cut-over, then at the interval the team sets (Learn recommends periodic drills), and after a database grows by the percentage the team sets

### TC-9: The restore requests fit the limits (design check, not drillable)
- Applies to: geo-restore
- Pass condition: two parts. Design check, not drillable: Learn gives no guarantee of capacity in the target region after a regional outage and the sharp increase of demand cannot be rehearsed, so the check passes when the runbook lists the databases in restore priority, the number of databases per subscription that need a restore at the same time is written next to the limit of 30 concurrently processed and 100 submitted restore requests (and 4 and 2,000 per elastic pool), the databases that cannot wait for a restore are marked for a failover group or active geo-replication instead, and a second DR region for the restore is written in the runbook. Measured: the restore duration per database of TC-8 is used to compute the total restore time of the databases in the order of the runbook and it is within the recovery time that the application owner accepts.
- How to measure: review the runbook against the inventory and the limits, and compute the total restore time from the durations of TC-8 and the number of concurrent restores; the capacity of the region can only be observed in a real regional outage.
- Evidence: the review record with its date, the table of databases with priority, size and measured duration, and the computed total.
- Frequency: after every change to the number or size of the databases, and yearly
